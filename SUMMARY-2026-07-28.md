# AgreementAtlas — what we did, 28 July

## The headline

**agreementatlas.com is live and demoable.** A visitor lands, clicks one button,
and gets a working three-document agreement family with a graph and five
questions that answer correctly. Before today they landed on an empty page
asking them to upload a contract — which your own terms tell them not to do.

---

## Shipped

**The public site.** Domain live over Cloudflare Tunnel, TLS, survives reboot.
Logo, wordmark, favicon, social-share card, sitemap, HSTS. Runs in a mode where
the shared library is switched off entirely, so your licensed vendor agreements
are not reachable — verified by fetching the site as a stranger and getting
nothing.

**A sample corpus that demonstrates the product.** Three fictional Northwind
documents, ~8,900 words: an EULA, a licence model schedule with five named-user
variants, and an order form with a real SKU and pricing table. Written so the
hard questions have checkable answers.

**Five demo questions, each tested against the live system.** The best one:

> *How many days per year can an Occasional Named User access InsightHub?*
> **52 — not the 40 in the schedule**, because the order form overrides it for
> that product only.

That answer requires reading one document over another. It is the thing a
single-document tool cannot do, and it is Ironclad's own problem domain.

**Conversation memory.** The assistant used to ask "which variant applies?" and
then answer your reply of "logical" with *"that is not a question."* It now
resolves the follow-up and shows what it understood.

**A working knowledge graph.** It was showing 7 of 152 nodes and the Fit button
could not fit — the layout inflated as nodes were added. Fixed.

## Found and fixed

**PDFs were being read wrong, everywhere, always.** Multi-column tables were
flattened line by line, so columns fused into single sentences. SAP's support
schedule produced *"…if normal business processes are seriously **and within 2
hours of case submission for SAP** affected"* — a response time welded into the
middle of a definition. Every clause drawn from a table has been quotable,
plausible and wrong for the life of the project. Now read properly.

**Over half the list items were never shown to the AI.** A clause like *"only be
used to support Licensee's use of the Software"* has no "may" or "must" of its
own — the modal lives in the sentence above it. A filter required one, so 693 of
1,220 such clauses were silently skipped. The model was never asked; the system
then displayed a deterministic guess as though it were analysis.

**The AI was being told to guess.** The instructions said to answer "OTHER" when
a sentence has no modal verb — but list items have none, and the answer format
offered no "other" option, so it had to pick. Rewritten.

**Definitions were being turned into obligations.** The model correctly marks a
definition as imposing no duty; the code discarded that judgement and forced an
obligation anyway. Now honoured.

**Document types the system could not recognise** — "transaction document",
"terms of service", "privacy policy", "support schedule" — were classified as
master agreements, which meant they competed for precedence with the terms they
sit beneath. Fixed. Loading SAP's real cloud agreement set found four of these.

## What the overnight audit found

An independent review read 217 clause/rule pairs individually, checked every
failure class it found against all 11,836 rules, and read 41 real commercial
software licences end to end. Two findings are larger than anything we had
flagged. I verified both myself before recording them.

**Rights are being silently deleted, not mislabelled.** A clause saying
*"Customer **can** request an on-site audit"* produces no rule at all — the code
handles "may" and forgets "can", so the proposition is discarded rather than
stored with a bad label. **131 of 440** such statements vanish. This is worse
than a wrong label because nothing is left to notice: the graph simply has no
record that the right exists. Salesforce's audit right is one of the missing
ones.

**The AI contradicts itself in 1 rule in 7.** 156 of 1,150 model-extracted rules
state a logically impossible pair — a prohibition that is positive, an
obligation that is negative. 106 are "prohibition + positive". The
non-AI path has **zero** of these, because there the two fields are derived from
each other. A wrong AI rule looks like a confident, complete record; a wrong
deterministic rule looks like a gap. **The confident one is the more dangerous.**

**Bad list-item linking is one in six.** 207 of 1,220 list items are attached to
the wrong parent clause, ranging from 7% of one vendor's documents to 59% of
another's — it tracks how cleanly their PDFs converted, not how they write
contracts. Two unrelated Adobe clauses produced the *same* fabricated rule.

**Reading 41 real licences showed what we would miss entirely** — things no
audit of existing records can reveal. Country-specific amendments (IBM carries
40+, each overriding a numbered section elsewhere), licences that are just a
pointer to another document (7 of the 41), a French-language warranty clause
that our English-only patterns cannot read, and flat-dollar liability caps that
appear in 8 of 41 commercial licences but are the rarest and most error-prone
category we have.

## Known and not yet fixed

**59 rules assert obligations the contract expressly denies.** *"shall be under
no obligation to maintain any Comments in confidence"* is stored as a positive
obligation. This is the worst defect we have: the graph stating the opposite of
the text, citably. Cause understood, fix planned.

**A third of what the AI extracts is labelled "OTHER".** Real contracts say
*agrees to*, *reserves the right*, *is entitled to* — none are modal verbs, so
the distinction between a discretionary right and a mandatory duty is lost.

**The clause anatomy panel is the weakest surface.** It is also the most
technical-looking. Tomorrow: make it right or hide it. For a sales conversation
it adds nothing, and a wrong label next to correct quoted text is the one thing
that turns "impressive" into "it doesn't work."

**Retrieval got measurably worse on OpenText** (MRR 0.646 → 0.561) while
extraction got better. Probable cause: better PDF reading produces fewer, larger
clauses, so a key phrase sits in a bigger chunk and ranks lower. Untested.

**Some list items are attached to the wrong parent clause**, which is upstream
of everything else and explains at least one nonsense label.

## Honest notes

Four times today a measurement nearly told the wrong story, and each was caught
only by checking the instrument rather than trusting the number:

- The retrieval benchmark was testing a code path the application never runs.
- A third of one quality metric turned out to be the measuring script's own bug.
- Two scripts read the fallback file rather than the pipeline that answers
  questions, so they could not see the defect that mattered.
- Every failed clause matched a rule I had just written, which looked like proof
  I had broken it — until the base rate showed 44 of 91 in the same population
  working fine.

Several predictions were refuted outright, including two of my own headline
ones. That is recorded rather than tidied away.

## For the job applications

The demo is in good shape for an HR screen or a sales hiring manager. The
precedence answer lands, the branding is professional, the local-first angle is
a real differentiator in CLM conversations right now.

Two cautions. **Frame it as "I built this to understand the problem", not as a
product** — bugs then read as expected, and the effort reads as initiative.
**And check the machine is awake before sending the link**, because it runs on
your Mac and a dead link is worse than no link.

What actually distinguishes it is not the graph. It is that the hard cases are
the *right* hard cases: allocation versus assignment, a licence needed for
someone who never logged in, an order form overriding a schedule. You do not
choose those examples unless you have lived in software licensing.
